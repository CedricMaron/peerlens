# REVIEW — Problem

Assess whether the problem is clearly stated, scientifically relevant, properly
scoped, motivated, and actually established.

## Criteria to assess

1. **Clarity** — can a reader in an adjacent field state the problem back
   correctly after one reading? Is it free of undefined jargon and vague
   qualifiers ("suboptimal", "challenging", "not well understood")?
2. **Scientific relevance** — does resolving this problem add knowledge, or only
   engineering convenience? Both can be valid, but they must not be conflated.
3. **Scope** — is the problem bounded enough to be addressable by one study?
   Is the boundary stated, or only implied?
4. **Motivation** — is the consequence of *not* solving it articulated?
5. **Establishment** — is there evidence the problem is real, or is it asserted?
   Distinguish: cited empirical evidence, the researcher's own observations,
   appeal to intuition, and bare assertion.
6. **Problem vs. solution separation** — is the problem stated independently of
   the researcher's chosen method? A problem defined as "no one has applied X"
   is a solution in disguise.
7. **Consistency with the rest of the research** — does the work that follows
   actually address *this* problem, or a narrower/different one?

## Section-specific failure modes to look for

- The "problem" is really the absence of the author's method.
- The problem is stated at a scale the study cannot possibly address, setting up
  overgeneralised claims later.
- Motivation rests on a practical concern (cost, latency, convenience) while the
  claims that follow are scientific.
- The problem's importance is supported only by citation-free assertion.
- The material describes an interesting phenomenon but never identifies what is
  problematic about it.

Mark `unknown` where the material genuinely does not say. Early-stage research
legitimately has an `incomplete` problem section; say what is missing rather
than inventing a problem on the researcher's behalf.
